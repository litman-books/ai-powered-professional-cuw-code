PROMPT BEFORE / AFTER -- the same job, two ways of asking
=========================================================

The job: get an AI assistant to draft an email announcing a change
to your customers. Same assistant, same day. The only difference is
how well you communicated what you actually wanted.

============================================================
BEFORE  --  the vague prompt (an incantation)
============================================================

    Write an email to my customers about the new system.

WHAT COMES BACK: a generic, hollow email. The assistant does not know
who you are, what the "new system" is, why customers should care, how
long the note should be, or what tone fits your practice. So it
guesses -- and with nothing to go on, it guesses bland, padded, and
faintly robotic. It may open with "In today's fast-paced world..."
and invent details you never gave it.

Garbage in, garbage out. The problem is not the assistant. The
problem is that no competent colleague could write a good email from
that instruction either.

============================================================
AFTER  --  the upgraded prompt (communication)
============================================================

    ROLE:
    You are an experienced office manager at a small dental practice
    who writes warm, plain-English messages to patients.

    OBJECTIVE:
    Draft a short email telling our patients that starting March 1 we
    are switching to a new online patient portal for scheduling and
    billing. Goal: patients feel reassured -- not alarmed -- and know
    the one thing they must do.

    CONTEXT:
    - Practice: Riverbend Family Dental. Signed "Maria, Office Manager."
    - Most patients are 40+ and not especially tech-savvy.
    - The only action required: click a setup link we email them the
      week of Feb 24, then set a password.
    - Their appointment history and insurance info carry over --
      nothing is lost.
    - We are proud this makes booking easier. We are NOT raising prices;
      do not imply that we are.

    EXAMPLES (tone to match):
    "Good news -- we've made a small change to make your life easier..."
    Warm, first-person-plural ("we"), no jargon, short paragraphs.

    FORMAT:
    - Subject line + body.
    - Under 180 words.
    - One short bulleted list of what changes.
    - End with a friendly sign-off and our phone number for questions.

WHAT COMES BACK NOW: a usable first draft in your voice, with the real
deadline, the one action, the reassurance, and the right length. You
will still read it with a cold eye and fix a line or two -- that is the
job -- but you are correcting a draft, not conjuring one from fog.

============================================================
THE POINT
============================================================
The difference between BEFORE and AFTER is not a secret phrase or a
magic word. It is that the second prompt told the assistant what a
competent colleague would need to know: who to be, what you want, the
facts it cannot guess, the tone, and the shape. That is not "prompt
engineering." That is professional communication -- the same skill
that makes you good at briefing a new hire.

You choose the tool. You own the verdict. Even the AFTER draft is a
draft. You decide what ships.
